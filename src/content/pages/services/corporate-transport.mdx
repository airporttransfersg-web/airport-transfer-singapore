## What is a corporate transport service?

A corporate transport service is a standing supply arrangement between a company and a chauffeured operator, under which trips are booked against an account, priced from an agreed rate card and invoiced in arrears. The individual booking looks like any other [Changi transfer](/services/changi-airport-transfers/) or [hourly chauffeur booking](/services/hourly-chauffeur/). What differs is everything around it: who is allowed to book, how the trip is coded, who receives the confirmation, and how the finance team sees it at month end.

The term overlaps with several others and buyers use them interchangeably. Executive transport usually means the same thing with an executive-class vehicle attached. Business chauffeur emphasises the driver. Corporate car service is the North American phrasing. Ground transportation management, which is what large multinationals put in a tender, describes a wider scope including reporting, duty of care and supplier consolidation. All of them start with an account.

What a corporate arrangement removes is reimbursement. When staff book their own rides, a company gets a stack of app receipts, no visibility of who travelled where, no way to tie a trip to a client matter, and no assurance that the vehicle was licensed and insured for a passenger who is travelling on company business. An account replaces all of that with one invoice, one supplier and one duty-of-care position.

## How a corporate account works

1. Account opening. Company details, billing contact, authorised bookers and the cost-code structure you want on the invoice. One form, no deposit.
2. Rate card agreement. Fixed rates by vehicle class for airport legs, hourly blocks and full days, held for the account term.
3. Booking. Authorised bookers email operations or use the form. Confirmations go to the booker and, if you want, to the passenger separately.
4. Travel. Driver name, mobile and plate go to the passenger the evening before. Flights are tracked without being asked.
5. Invoicing. One monthly invoice, itemised by date, passenger, route and cost code, with a trip report alongside it.

Changes to authorised bookers, cost codes or passenger lists go through the same operations contact rather than a portal.

Executive assistants and team assistants. The EA books, the director travels, and the EA is accountable for whether the car was there. Confirmations arrive by email with the flight number attached, changes go by email, and nobody has to install anything. The monthly invoice reconciles without chasing receipts.

Finance and procurement teams. The buying problem is visibility and duty of care: which staff travelled, in whose vehicle, under what insurance, coded to which project. A single supplier with LTA-licensed vehicles, PDVL drivers and a coded monthly invoice answers all four questions in one document.

Investment banks and law firms on transaction work. Roadshows and due diligence run to a schedule that changes hourly, across Raffles Place, Marina Bay Financial Centre and Suntec, often with confidentiality attached. Drivers on these accounts sign a confidentiality undertaking and are briefed on stops rather than told them on the day.

Technology and professional services firms with visiting teams. Regular inbound arrivals from the region, several people on the same flight, and a preference for one Business Van over three separate cars. Standing arrangements handle the repeat pattern.

Hotels, family offices and embassies. The booker is a concierge or a protocol officer, the passenger changes constantly, and discretion matters more than the rate. Vehicle standards and driver presentation are set once and applied to every job.

Companies relocating staff. Airport arrival, temporary housing viewings, school visits and the first month of commuting all sit under the same account, coded to the relocation budget rather than to travel.

Where an account loses: for one-off trips by staff who rarely travel, an app is simpler and probably cheaper. Accounts pay off on repetition, on airport work and on anything a booker has to answer for.

## What to know before opening an account

What credit terms are available?
Credit terms are set on approval. New accounts can start on prepayment and move to credit once the billing pattern is established.

Can the invoice be split by department?
Yes. Every trip carries a cost centre, project code or matter number captured at booking, and the invoice groups by whichever field you choose.

Is there a minimum spend?
No minimum to open. Volume terms are reviewed once an account is running regular monthly volume.

Can we restrict who books?
Yes. Only named authorised bookers can charge to the account, and the list is changed by the billing contact.

What about duty of care and insurance?
Every vehicle is LTA-licensed for private hire and carries commercial passenger insurance. Every driver holds a PDVL, which requires a medical and a background check. Certificates are provided at account opening for your records.

Do you sign NDAs?
Yes, at company level, and drivers assigned to the account sign a confidentiality undertaking covering passenger identity, destinations and conversation.

Can passengers book directly against the account?
Yes, if you authorise them individually. Most accounts keep booking with assistants and let passengers make changes only.

What reporting do we get?
A monthly trip report by date, passenger, route, vehicle class and cost code, in a spreadsheet alongside the invoice.

Peak periods to plan around?
Grand Prix weekend in September, the Singapore Airshow in February, Fintech Festival week in November, Chinese New Year, and the two weeks before Christmas. Standing bookings are protected in these windows; ad-hoc requests may not be.

## Mistakes companies make with ground transport

1. Buying on rate alone. The cheapest hourly rate rarely includes waiting, tolls and parking, and the invoice arrives with three extra lines.
2. Leaving airport arrivals to the passenger. A director in a pickup queue after a 13-hour flight is the most visible failure in the whole travel programme.
3. Booking legs for a roadshow day. Hourly is cheaper from three stops and keeps one driver on the schedule.
4. Not fixing cost codes at booking. Coding at month end from a route list is guesswork.
5. Ignoring insurance and licensing. Staff travelling on company business in an unlicensed vehicle is a duty-of-care exposure, not a saving.
6. Booking peak weeks late. During Grand Prix and Airshow weeks the fleet is committed well in advance across the whole island.
7. Treating relocation trips as travel spend. Arrivals, viewings and school runs belong to the relocation budget and code differently.
8. Letting every team book its own supplier. Three suppliers means three insurance positions and no volume discount.

## Licensing, insurance and confidentiality

Vehicles are licensed by the Land Transport Authority as private hire cars and inspected annually for commercial use. Drivers hold the Private Hire Car Driver's Vocational Licence, the PDVL, which requires a medical examination, a criminal record check and a licensing course. Commercial passenger insurance is carried and certificates are provided at account opening.

Corporate clients are concentrated in financial services, law, technology and hospitality. Drivers assigned to corporate accounts sign a confidentiality undertaking. The fleet runs five classes, from a Premium MPV to a 40-seater coach, and is shared with the chauffeur operation at MySingaporeDriver.
